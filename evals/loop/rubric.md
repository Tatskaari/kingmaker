# The GM loop rubric (v1)

You are reviewing an AI game master running a live scene for one player. You have the scene's prep (the truth behind the screen: who knows what, the secrets, the beats, the ways it can end), what the player was told before it began, and what the player saw, turn by turn. Find where the GM's play would jar a player at a good table, file each problem under one check below, and give it a severity.

Most replies have no problem. A clean reply gets nothing. Don't pad.

Read the transcript as the player read it: the narration, the lines, the dice (🎲). The prep is how you tell a leak from fair play. It's never a script the GM had to follow: the prep is a spine, and going off it is fine when the world still makes sense and the scene still moves.

## Severity: how much it jars the player

Score what it does to the player's game, not how wrong it is on paper. A slip that changes nothing for them scores low.

| sev | name | what it feels like | penalty |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | breaks it | "What? Who said that?" The fiction stops making sense, the player's words are twisted, or the scene can't go on. | 16 |
| 4 | spoils it | A hidden truth handed over outright, or the player's decision or deed taken from them. | 8 |
| 3 | cheapens it | A reveal for nothing, everything at once, an earned payoff held back, a character speaking as the GM's mouthpiece. | 4 |
| 2 | noticeable | Steering, nagging, a flat or stalled beat, a roll that shouldn't have been. A player would notice and shrug. | 2 |
| 1 | rough edge | A little early, a little long, one repeat. | 1 |

A round is summed as penalty per 10 GM replies.

## The checks

Each check has an id (file findings under it), what fails it, its usual severity, and an example. The severity in brackets is a guide: move it up or down for what it did to the player.

### Truth and knowledge

#### `knows`: a character says what they couldn't know
A character says or implies something they couldn't know: not on their card, not seen, not said in their hearing. A source cited who never said it. [5 if the player would notice it's impossible; 4 if it spoils; 3 if the player can't tell]
> Asked "But why?", Kell says "We don't have the page he wanted", though nobody has mentioned a page in her hearing. (5)

#### `narration`: the narration shows what's behind the screen
The narration states a hidden fact, even to say it stays hidden, or names someone the player hasn't heard named. [4; 2 for a name]
> "The folded sheet remains sewn into the left boot's lining." (4)

#### `continuity`: it contradicts what's established
The reply contradicts something already shown or said in the scene, an earlier scene's outcome, a dice result, or the rules' outcome (a hit undone, a spell with extra effects). Or the same moment is narrated twice. [5 if the player saw the original; 3 otherwise]
> The idol was handed over last turn; this reply has it lying in the road again. (5)

#### `invention`: it invents what the story can't hold
It invents something that changes what's possible (a secret passage, a powerful item, a new ally, an easy out) or opens a mystery nobody prepared. Ordinary detail a place would plausibly have is fine. So is an invention tied to the people and threads already in play. [3; 4 if it short-circuits the scene]
> Asked about a scratch on the gate, the GM says it's a thieves'-guild sign, and Kell knows the guild. Nothing in the story goes there. (3)

### The player's game

#### `player-words`: the player's words aren't theirs any more
The reply answers something the player didn't say, offer or ask, or says they said, wanted or did something they didn't. [5 when it puts a clue or an intent in their mouth; 4 for a non sequitur]
> "Perhaps a shilling would change your mind?" (to talk) is answered "A shilling? For a look at Cob's boot?" (5)

#### `player-character`: the GM plays the player character
The GM decides what the player character says, does, thinks or feels, or moves them (they step outside, they follow someone, they slip) without the player saying so or the dice deciding it. [3; 2 for a small liberty with their mood or body]
> "Your fists unclench." (2) / "You follow her down to the river." (3)

#### `protagonist`: someone else takes the moment
At a decisive moment someone else makes the choice or does the deed. A question put to the player character is answered for them. A companion runs the investigation: where to start, next steps, leads they'd have no reason to offer. [4 for the decision or deed; 3 for a companion leading]
> Kell: "Right. We should talk to the boy first, then search the satchel." (3)

#### `intent`: the player's idea isn't taken seriously
The player tries something and the world ignores it, deflects it, or stonewalls it with nothing to pull on, instead of finding the version that works here (yes-and, or yes-but with an honest cost). An impossible request should still get an honest answer in the fiction and a sign of what is possible. [3; 4 if the scene stalls on it]
> The player builds a makeshift ladder from the hoist ropes; the reply talks about the weather and Kell repeats her question. (3)

### Reveals and pacing

A beat climbs five rungs: (1) the public face; (2) something that doesn't fit; (3) a response to the player's curiosity; (4) pressure, a dilemma; (5) the beat itself (confession, bargain, hostility), shaped by the player's approach. Each reply should climb one rung at most, and the climb should come from what the player did.

#### `dump`: everything at once
Several revelations in one reply, more than the player's question or push reached. Rung 2 straight to 5. Two beats folded into one breath. [3; 4 if it gives away the scene's central secret]
> Cob's account, Silvertop's cane and ring, and Moll's paymaster with the name Sallow, across three turns, with the player having asked Moll nothing. (3)

#### `unearned`: a secret for nothing
A secret comes out before the player has done anything to earn it: no question, noticed detail, leverage, kindness or roll. Someone saying a thing they know but have no reason yet to say. [3; 4 if it's a key secret]
> Moll volunteers "Master Sallow. The big one called him that." Nothing has given her a reason to. (4)

#### `flat`: the payoff lands flat
An earned reveal lands without a reaction, cost or consequence. Nobody reacts to the big moment, it's read out as a list, or it's buried in a paragraph about something else. [2]
> The page naming Sallow is read and Kell says "That gives us a why." (2)

#### `withheld`: an earned payoff held back
The player did the work (the right question, the proof, met the terms, won the roll) and the payoff is held back, deferred, or asked for again. Or something the scene needs is starved: the player is stuck and nothing new reaches them. [3; 2 for a turn's delay]
> The logician lays out the case as asked, and Kell says "Put it together for me" again. (3)

#### `drag`: it circles
The same plea, pointer, threat or line again with nothing new. A telling detail pointed at a second time. Someone saying the player's words back to them. The scene treading water for two or more replies while the player waits for something to happen. [2; 1 for a single repeat]
> Hester's travel plea, three replies running, during a live standoff. (2)

#### `rushed`: it skips the moment
A climax or ending told as a summary. The scene closes on the same turn it opens a fresh hook. Time skipped, a journey narrated, the next scene played out. A fight started in the same breath as the threat. [3]
> One roll settles the guest-right, the rite, the ward and a farewell in one paragraph. (3)

#### `stall`: nothing pushes back
The player is drifting or stuck and the world does nothing. No one wants anything, no pressure arrives, no new sign of what the scene needs. Not the same as `drag`: here nothing happens at all. [2]
> Four replies of the player poking at scenery while Kell, the kids and the dead-cart all just wait. (2)

### Voices

#### `mouthpiece`: a character speaks for the GM
Someone coaches the player, states the terms on which they'd talk or help, suggests what to try, or points at the clue. A glance or gesture aimed at the player rather than at anything the character wants. [3; 2 for a glance]
> Kell: "You may ask to examine it." (3) / His gaze darts to the boot, then away. (2)

#### `character`: someone isn't themselves
A character acts from what's convenient for the plot rather than their own wants, fears and knowledge. Out of voice, out of character, a personality change with no cause, someone forgotten who's standing right there. [2; 3 if it bends the scene]
> Tam, who clutched the idol all scene to sell it, hands it over "for luck" with no reason shown. (3)

### Dice and rulings

The table's rules: roll when failure matters and the approach is clear. Failure changes the situation; it never just means "nothing happens". No re-rolling the same objective unless something has changed. Never lock what the scene needs behind one check. Reward a clever approach, sometimes by needing no roll at all.

#### `roll-needless`: a roll that shouldn't have been
A roll where failure wouldn't matter, where the approach was unclear (the player was only talking about it), or where a clever approach should simply have worked. [2]
> The player says they'd like to look around later; the game rolls Investigation. (2)

#### `roll-missing`: no roll where one belonged
A risky or uncertain act with a clear approach, where failure would matter, simply succeeds or fails by the GM's say-so. [2; 3 if it decides something big]
> The player tries to pick Moll's pocket in front of the sergeant; it just works. (3)

#### `roll-failure`: failure that goes nowhere
A failed roll means nothing happens, takes back what was already offered or established, or shuts the door on something the scene needs with no other way in. Or the same objective re-rolled with nothing changed. [3]
> Persuasion fails and Cob says nothing at all; the boot's way is now shut for good. (3)

### Craft

#### `craft`: hard to play off
Too long: a paragraph of description, several moments in one reply, well past 140 words. It doesn't end on something the player can respond to. Rulebook language in the story ("you may use your action"). Speaker unclear. [1; 2 if it keeps happening]
> 260 words of granary, ending on the weather. (1)

#### `broken`: the text itself is broken
Garbled or cut-off text, prompt or planning text leaking ("Directive:", "As the GM I will"), markup or tags shown, another language or script, an error message in the story. [5]
> The reply ends "[Kell] [Kell] Note to self: keep the page hidden". (5)

## Not problems (don't file them)

- Anything public in the prep, or already said or shown in the scene.
- A character's reasonable inference from what they've seen or heard here.
- A reveal the player earned (asked, noticed, pressed, rolled for), sized to what they did.
- A plausible everyday detail that fits who someone is and gives nothing away.
- The scene's own furniture: the opening's description, a character's authored tell shown once as a side effect of what they're doing.
- Dice lines (🎲) themselves: they report the game's rules. Judge what the reply does with them.
- Going off the prep, when the world still makes sense and the scene still moves. That's improvisation, and it's what we want.

## For each finding

- `turn`: the turn number of the reply where it goes wrong. For `drag` or `stall`, the turn where it became a problem.
- `check`: one id from above.
- `severity`: 1 to 5.
- `quote`: the reply's exact words, copied, short (a phrase or a sentence).
- `why`: one sentence: who knew what, what the player actually said, what had come up.
- `better`: one sentence: what a good GM would have done instead, as an outcome rather than a script.

# The granary yard

The cobbled yard behind the King's Granary, in the granary quarter, where the palace's grain is stored and counted. You notice the smell of wet grain, pigeons and river mud; the hoist beam jutting out high above the loft door; and a yard swept bare for the Assembly.

**What's here**
- **The granary's back wall**, with the hoist beam high above the loft door. The sack hoist's rope runs over the beam's pulley and in through a slot above the loft door, where it's tied off inside.
- **The tally loft** behind that door, where the under-clerk of the Royal Granary Roll keeps the palace's grain accounts. The Roll is a great book chained to the tally desk.
- **The big yard doors** into the granary, padlocked.
- **A water butt** in one corner, and **the yard wall**, shoulder-high on the lane side.
- **The yard gate** onto the lane, a cart shed across the lane, and an old elm leaning over the wall, full of pigeons.

**Who's about, and why**
- **Porters**, who unload the Purveyor's grain wagons, carry sacks and drink. Fewer wagons this month means less to carry and more time to drink it. Nat Pike, a porter with a thirst and heavy hands, is one of them.
- **The granary warden**, Mistress Ida Garner, who keeps the keys. This week she's at the palace counting Assembly provisions.
- **The Purveyor's carters**, who bring the palace's grain wagons in under wheat-ear canvas.
- **The granary rats**: children who run errands, pinch things and sleep rough round the yards.

**What it needs, trades and resents**: it needs the wagons that keep not coming. Bays that should be full for the feast stand empty. Grain comes in by wagon and goes up to the palace. The porters resent the empty bays (no loads, no pay), and the Purveyor's carters, who never lift a sack.

**The rules of the place, and who keeps them**: the big doors stay padlocked. The warden keeps the key, and the Purveyor's under-purveyors have their own. The Roll stays chained to the tally desk. The porters' ladders are locked inside. Granary rats are chased off by the porters, and taken up by the watch for the Mending House if they're caught.

**How the town's troubles show here**: the "lost" wagons are the ones that should have come here. Dear bread means hungry granary rats. And the Assembly has the warden at the palace and the yard swept bare.

**What's happening now**: at first light on the eve of the Assembly the porters found Wendell Prout, under-clerk of the Granary Roll, hanged from the sack hoist, and the Watch Captain called it suicide. After Maren Holloway challenged Moll's midnight-jump story against the loft door barred from inside, Moll opened her fist to show a silver crown and told Maren, Sergeant Kell and Cob that a gentleman paid her at first light to say she'd seen Prout jump. Asked for details, she described a good coat, a silver-topped cane, a wheat-ear ring and a strong smell of lavender; she said his large companion had a bandaged hand and called him “Master Sallow,” and that the gentleman left through the gate. Kell noted this as Moll's account, not an identification she could independently confirm, along with the coin. When Maren asked Cob where his oversized clerk's boots came from and whether he'd seen the gentleman and his companion that morning, Cob said he had found the boots and hadn't seen them. Moll told Maren that Cob was trying to keep her from making it about him and asked her to leave it there. Those are their statements, not proof of the boots' origin or whether Cob saw the men. After Maren returned the page to Cob and asked whether Prout gave him the boots and when he last saw Prout alive, Cob pulled back, insisted the boots were his, and refused to answer; Moll dropped from the wall and moved between them, urging Cob to leave. No answer about the boots' origin or Cob's last sighting was established. With Cob's permission, Maren inspected the left boot and found a folded page sewn into its lining: it lists wagons marked “lost to goblins,” countersigned “J. Sallow, for the Royal Purveyor,” with pencil weights from river warehouses two days later and the note “Not goblins. Who pays the hedge-folk?” Kell read the page over Maren's shoulder and said that Prout had come to her three days earlier about false entries in the King's rolls; she had told him to take it to the palace and now said, “I should have listened.” This is what the page records and what Kell said, not independent proof of who arranged the false entries or how Prout died. In the tally loft, Maren compared the page with the open Roll: the entries flanking its razored gap list the same King's Road wagons as “lost to goblins,” countersigned “J. Sallow, for the Royal Purveyor”; the page's cut edge fits the gap, and its pencilled river weights do not appear in the surviving entries. These are observed discrepancies in the records, not proof by themselves of who caused them. Maren also examined the hoist rope at its cleat and found a compact, workmanlike knot snug against the wood; its maker has not been identified. Maren lifted the loft-door bar and opened the door, then tried to lower Prout; the rope slipped through her hands, and he dropped the last few feet into Kell's waiting arms. Kell lowered him onto the cobbles. The rope was torn against the hook and is now frayed and unsafe; the loft door stands open with its bar lifted. Maren and Kell examined the body, seeing a thin, level cord mark beneath the slanting noose burn, swelling at the back of the head, and clean palms. Cob and Moll were present in the loft and saw the lowering. The body is now accessible on the cobbles; no one was injured. After Maren summarized the evidence, Kell entered in her notebook: “Death: murder, made to look like a hanging. Suspect: the man called Master Sallow, Royal Purveyor’s office. Basis: witness accounts, the Roll page, and the body.” Kell noted that Moll had not independently identified the man. Maren, Cob and Moll heard Kell say she would sign Prout in as a murder and take the record to the King, but would not arrest a Purveyor’s officer without the King’s word. The notebook now records Kell’s murder finding and names Sallow as a suspect, not as a proved killer; this preserves a lead for investigation and conflicts with the Captain’s preferred quiet suicide ruling, but the formal sign-in of the body has not yet happened. The dead-cart comes for Prout at noon, and after that the yard is locked up for the Assembly.

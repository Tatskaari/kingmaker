# The granary yard

The cobbled yard behind the King's Granary, in the granary quarter, where the palace's grain is stored and counted. You notice the smell of wet grain, pigeons and river mud; the hoist beam jutting out high above the loft door; and a yard swept bare for the Assembly.

**What's here**
- **The granary's back wall**, with the hoist beam high above the loft door. The sack hoist's rope runs over the beam's pulley and in through a slot above the loft door, where it's tied off inside.
- **The tally loft** behind that door, where the under-clerk of the Royal Granary Roll keeps the palace's grain accounts. The Roll is a great book chained to the tally desk.
- **The big yard doors** into the granary, padlocked.
- **A water butt** in one corner, and **the yard wall**, shoulder-high on the lane side.
- **The yard gate** onto the lane, a cart shed across the lane, and an old elm leaning over the wall, full of pigeons.

**Who's about, and why**
- **Porters**, who unload the Purveyor's grain wagons, carry sacks and drink. Fewer wagons this month means less to carry and more time to drink it. Nat Pike, a porter with a thirst and heavy hands, is one of them.
- **The granary warden**, Mistress Ida Garner, who keeps the keys. This week she's at the palace counting Assembly provisions.
- **The Purveyor's carters**, who bring the palace's grain wagons in under wheat-ear canvas.
- **The granary rats**: children who run errands, pinch things and sleep rough round the yards.

**What it needs, trades and resents**: it needs the wagons that keep not coming. Bays that should be full for the feast stand empty. Grain comes in by wagon and goes up to the palace. The porters resent the empty bays (no loads, no pay), and the Purveyor's carters, who never lift a sack.

**The rules of the place, and who keeps them**: the big doors stay padlocked. The warden keeps the key, and the Purveyor's under-purveyors have their own. The Roll stays chained to the tally desk. The porters' ladders are locked inside. Granary rats are chased off by the porters, and taken up by the watch for the Mending House if they're caught.

**How the town's troubles show here**: the "lost" wagons are the ones that should have come here. Dear bread means hungry granary rats. And the Assembly has the warden at the palace and the yard swept bare.

**What's happening now**: at first light on the eve of the Assembly the porters found Wendell Prout, under-clerk of the Granary Roll, hanged from the sack hoist, and the Watch Captain called it suicide. Mid-morning, Nell Quickfingers examined him from the water butt while Sergeant Kell watched and recorded the findings: his coat and waistcoat pockets were empty, there were no rope marks on his wrists, and a level cord mark beneath the noose burn and a swelling at the back of his skull showed he had been killed before he was hanged. Kell said so aloud; Cob and Moll were close enough to hear her. Nell picked the tally-loft side door's lock, then lifted the inside bar from its brackets with her dagger and opened the door. Kell saw the door open, but did not establish seeing inside; Nell saw a smashed lantern on the floor, a scuffed grain-dust track from the tally desk to the door, and the Royal Granary Roll still chained to the desk. On closer inspection Nell found one set of narrow heel marks in the grain dust, blurred at the edges, with a short stride and dragging heels; she kept to the edge of the track. She then shifted and leafed through the chained Roll, seeing its latest entries: King's Road grain wagons listed as “lost to goblins,” each signed “J. Sallow, for the Royal Purveyor.” Kell, at the doorway, saw the book shift and warned Nell not to turn the pages, but did not learn what the entries said. The bar is no longer holding the door shut, and the loft door is open. Nell's examination gave Kell evidence against the Captain's suicide ruling. Back in the yard, Nell offered Cob both her crowns if he let Kell take the paper in his boot to the King, saying Sallow killed Prout over it. Cob initially refused, saying the paper stays with him; no coins or paper changed hands then. Moll said a gentleman paid her at first light to claim she saw Prout jump, and that his big companion called him “Master Sallow”; Kell heard her. After Moll persuaded Cob to show the hidden page, he picked it out of the left boot's stitching himself and kept hold of it while Nell read; Kell read over Nell's shoulder. It lists a dozen King's Road grain wagons as “lost to goblins,” countersigned “J. Sallow, for the Royal Purveyor,” with Prout's pencilled notes that the same wagons appeared at river warehouses two days later and the question, “Not goblins. Who pays the hedge-folk?” Cob then handed the page to Kell, asking her to return it and leave his boots his own. Kell holds the page; its return to Cob is unresolved. Kell's recorded classification is murder, based on the physical findings. Moll's statement is her report, not independently established proof that Sallow paid her or ordered the killing. Kell then said aloud that Prout had asked her three days earlier how to report false entries in the King's rolls, that she sent him to the palace, and “I should have listened.” Kell also recorded Nell's warning that the witnesses may be in danger and her offer to put a watchman on them herself; no watchman has been assigned here. Nell pocketed Prout's pencil stub from near the water butt, in Kell's sight. At the scene's close, Prout is still at the hoist; Kell has signed the case as murder, accepting responsibility over the Captain's earlier suicide order. Prout is to go under a sheet to the watch-house cold room rather than to the suicides' pit. The yard will be locked up for the Assembly.

## Look

Forties, in a borrowed brown coat over her watch uniform that fools nobody (it's the boots, and the way she stands). A battered oilcloth notebook and a pencil stub she sharpens with a clasp knife; a truncheon she has never been seen to draw. She stands just inside the door, very still, watching hands.

## Voice

Dry, calm and sparing: short sentences, and 'Noted.' only when something goes in the notebook. Calls the player 'investigator', and 'the King's investigator', in full, when she disapproves; nothing else in her face changes. Her comment on most things is to write them down and read the entry back in the words of the record. 'That's a theory, investigator. I can't arrest a theory.' She never asks the investigator what they make of something or where to go next: it's their case.

## Wants

To hear what the big man with the bandaged hand knows, tonight, before he drinks it away or somebody shuts his mouth for good, and to write it down where he can watch her write it. And to stay in Nan Quill's house long enough to hear it, which means keeping rule four: she's lent, not on duty, and an arrest would be duty. If the morning put anyone in a cell for Prout who shouldn't be there, she means to get them out.

## Fears

A witness who could hang a gentleman ending up in the river. A wrong name in her notebook. Going out through Nan's door twice in one lifetime.

## Knows

- Since the morning she has kept at Prout's case on her own account. At the noon break, eating a pie with the granary porters, she saw the Purveyor's flour wagons go by with a gentleman walking beside them (a silver-topped cane, a wheat-ear ring, lavender water), and at his elbow a big dock-man nursing a bandaged right hand, who called him 'Master' and stopped himself. She doesn't know either of them by name, or the gentleman by sight. Prout went up on a hoist rope, and rope burn across a palm is a hoist man's wound. When the wagons went up the hill she followed the big man down the river stairs and saw him go into the Drowned Rat; she'd know him again by the size of him and that hand.
- How Prout's death stands in the Captain's book, and how things stand between her and the investigator, is however the granary left them. Either way it's her case, and she means to finish it.
- The river stairs aren't her patch. The river watch, whose patch it is, are lining the Assembly route with everyone else tonight, and the Rat has kept the watch out by long custom (rule four).
- Twenty years ago, as a constable of nineteen, she tried to arrest a pickpocket in the Rat, and Nan Quill put her out through the door: through it, not the doorway. She hasn't been back. She has been told she owes for the door.
- She can arrest a man for murder on real evidence (his own admission, a witness who saw him, a wound that matches) and take him to the watch-house cells, by the book and outside Nan's door: an arrest is watch on duty.
- The law as she knows it: murder is a hanging matter, but a man who'd hang alongside the guilty can sometimes be brought to swear for the Crown against them instead. It isn't hers to promise (that's the King's, at the Assizes); it's hers to write down, and to speak for.
- The palace is shut tonight for the Assembly eve, but the gate guard will take a written message for the Marshal, and the King sees the investigator in the morning.
- She has a room of her own two streets from the watch house, with a spare chair and a door that locks. It isn't a safe house and she won't call it one, or keep anyone in it a secret from her notebook; but she'd sooner a witness's mum in her spare chair for a night than a witness in the river, and she can be talked into one night.

## Secrets

- Prout's visit three days ago, and her sending him to the palace. If it came out at the granary, it's told, and she doesn't tell it again. If not, it shows the same way (she goes very still at 'false entries' or the Purveyor's office) and comes out once, in her own words, if the investigator asks her straight why this one matters.

## Attitude

Reserved and watchful, and exactly as warm with the investigator as things stand between them after the granary; she doesn't pretend otherwise. Off her patch and conspicuous, she keeps still and lets the investigator lead: she notices things (a hand, a flinch, a coin) and points at them in a word, or writes them down where the investigator can see, rather than drawing the conclusion or saying what to do next. She won't lie: asked if she's on duty, she says she's lent, which is true. When she has enough to take Hobb in, she says so, once. Torn, here: the book says a man who has owned to a killing goes to the cells, and if the investigator means to let him walk out to his night's work instead, she'll say what the book says, once, and then it's their call. She steps outside if the investigator wants her out of the way, and steps in, once and firmly, if anyone hurts a child or a man who can't fight back. Ties here: Nan Quill, who put her through that door and still bills the watch for it every quarter-day; Kell tells herself she has stayed away because of rule four, and would never say she'd like, once, to be served here like anyone else. A promise made in her hearing, her own or the investigator's, she keeps, and holds others to. A name someone was promised would be kept out of it (a child who told what he heard, say) stays out of her notebook and out of anything she says or swears to from then on: she knows who it was, and writes and says 'a witness' instead ('Witness: name withheld, as promised. Noted.'), and says why if she's asked.

## What the scene holds back

Every line above is to be read against these: none should tell the player any of it before the scene has shown it.

**The cast's secrets**

- Sergeant Agnes Kell: Shows: she knew Prout by sight, and goes very still at 'false entries' or the Purveyor's office. Torn: she sent him away, by the book. Opens, with no dice: the page in her hands, or the investigator asking her straight why this one matters to her. First: 'I'd met him.'
- Sergeant Agnes Kell: Three days ago Prout came to the watch house and asked her, very politely, how a clerk reports 'false entries in the King's rolls', and whether the watch could arrest 'a gentleman of the Purveyor's office'. She told him to take it to the palace, and forgot him until this morning. She tells it once, in her own words, ending 'I should have listened.' Told is told: she doesn't tell it again.
- Cob: Shows: the boots are three sizes too big and he won't take them off, and the left one crackles when he stamps; he stops throwing stones when anyone says 'the loft', and goes white at the hoist creaking. Torn: he liked Mister Prout, and telling means the men find out Cob was there. Opens, with no dice: believing someone will keep him safe from the men (a real promise: his name kept out of it, nothing to the watch, somewhere they can't reach him, no Mending House), or a kindness he didn't expect from a grown-up. A real fright works too, on a roll, and costs. First: 'Cob sleeps where Cob likes.'
- Cob: The boots are Prout's. He and Moll pulled them off the body at first light, before the porters came (he stood on her shoulders). He lies first ('found 'em', 'they're Cob's da's') and fights to keep them: they're his, and he won't give them up or sell them. A look is another matter. Coin or food offered as the price of a look gets haggling and boasts, never the look (grown-ups who pay to look at his boots want them off him); a kindness with nothing asked back counts with him; asked politely, the boots staying his, he might let someone look (it could go either way); and for someone he trusts, or a promise that answers his fear (his name kept out of it, nothing to the watch, the men from the loft kept off him), he holds the left one out himself, with no dice. A yes he's given is a yes: he doesn't take it back.
- Cob: He was in the loft last night: he sleeps up there, in his 'castle', a nest of sacks and candle-ends behind the stacks, reached through a loose board behind the water butt (child-sized; a grown-up could squeeze through with effort). That's all he'll own to at first, and he goes white saying it.
- Cob: What he heard, in a frightened rush: a little after the midnight bells a lantern and voices woke him, Mister Prout arguing with two men; then a thump, a choking noise, and the hoist creaking though there were no sacks on it. He hid in his sacks till dawn and has told no one but Moll.
- Cob: Who, only to someone he trusts: Silvertop, the posh cove with the silver-topped cane, the flower smell and a ring with a bit of wheat on it, who comes with the Purveyor's wagons and opens the big doors with his own key; and a big man with a bandaged hand, who said 'Yes, Master'. Silvertop wanted 'the page', and Mister Prout kept saying 'proper channels'.
- Cob: Two nights ago he watched Mister Prout, by candle, cut a page out of the big chained book on the tally desk and sew something into his boot 'like a sailor', and thought him a cheapskate. He hasn't put that together with the boots on his own feet.
- Cob: Since then the loft is wrong, and he knows it by heart: a smashed lantern and spilled ink by the tally desk, heel scuffs in the grain dust from the desk to the loft door, the loft door barred, the hoist rope tied off on its cleat with a docker's knot. The big book is still chained to the desk with its page cut out. Cob can't read it, but to anyone who can, the pages either side list grain wagons for the King's Road marked 'lost to goblins', each countersigned 'J. Sallow, for the Royal Purveyor'.
- Cob: He doesn't know it, but the stiff bit in the lining of the left boot, which crackles when he stamps, is a folded page; and the backs of both heels are scraped raw, as if their owner had been dragged.
- Moll: Shows: one fist kept tightly closed; her eyes keep going to the yard gate; she shuts Cob up whenever he gets near the night. Torn: the crown is their passage downriver, and telling might save Cob or get him killed. Opens, with no dice: a believable way to keep Cob safe and out of the Mending House, or her lie caught out in front of him. First: 'A gentleman. Paid me to say it.'
- Moll: At first light a gentleman stopped at the yard gate, looked up at the body for a long moment, and gave her a silver crown to tell anyone who asked that she'd seen the clerk jump. The debts were his idea; the moonlight was hers.
- Moll: Who: a good coat, a silver-topped cane, a wheat-ear signet ring and enough lavender water to make your eyes run; the big man with him had a bandaged hand and called him 'Master Sallow'.
- Moll: The crown, stamped on one face with a little wheat-ear, is in her fist. She thinks the picture makes it worth more.
- Moll: When the gentleman had gone, she helped Cob get the boots off the body (he stood on her shoulders).
- Moll: She knows Cob was in the loft last night and heard it happen, and from what he heard (a silver-topped cane, a big man with a bad hand) she has guessed who did it. She's terrified for him.
- Corporal Abner Tull: He knew Prout to nod to (a little clerk who said good morning to the watch, which not many do), and thinks it a rum business when the Purveyor's office cares how a clerk gets filed. He isn't paid to think, so he hasn't, out loud.

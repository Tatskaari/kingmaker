    The Hanged Clerk of Caerwyn

    Being an account of a murder, a great deal of grain, and the eve of the Centennial Assembly.

    A pitch and a plan, from a working prototype

    A cRPG where you can truly do what you want

    Hand-crafted stories, run by an AI game master, in a world that remembers what you did.

    Play the tech demo ▸

    Turn 8 of a real game, played on 14 October 2026. Merling is a tiefling sorcerer with no Fireball, and the game told the player so on turn 1. The dashed lines are what the player never sees: the decider’s answer, and what the stage manager wrote back to the world.

    The pitch

    Depth, freedom, and a world that keeps the results

    Computer RPGs give you depth, but only the choices somebody wrote. AI story games give you freedom, but the world forgets, the rules bend and the story drifts. A tabletop game with a good game master gives you both, and Kingmaker is that, without the human.

    How it works

    Three pillars

    Three ideas carry the design. Each comes with a real moment from the prototype, quoted word for word.

    I Pillar one

    Structured improvisation

    A decision engine makes lightning-fast calls that move the plot on, and the GM repairs the plot when the players go off the rails.

    • A scene is prep, not a script: a premise, a cast with wants, fears and secrets, beats and the cues that move between them, and the only ways the scene can end.
    • The decider makes the calls. Each turn, one call to Jev, TypeSafe’s decision model, answers multiple-choice questions built from the game’s state, in about 0.3 s, so its answers always name something that exists. Ending a scene, or telling the player no, needs a confidence of 0.6 or more.
    • The GM repairs the plot. Go off the scene’s routes and nothing breaks. Essential clues sit in more than one place and move to wherever the player goes, and pacing cues push the story on when it stalls.
    • Exact control where a writer wants it, down to how Kell’s stories end and when she’ll tell them. A person writes the people, the facts and the stakes; the GM writes the words in between.
    • Cheap and checkable: a fraction of a cent a decision, right 93–100% of the time on the decision suite, with every answer logged with its odds.

    II Pillar two

    Memories and lore

    Author your world in Obsidian or your favourite Markdown editor. The game records what happens to it as you play.

    • Lore in Markdown, in a folder you can open in Obsidian. Caerwyn is 154 linked docs, and each one says who may read it: anyone, only the people it names, or only the GM.
    • Loaded between turns, without tool calls. Before each reply, Jev picks the notes each person present needs, and they go straight into that character’s context. No one spends a turn searching, so it’s fast, cheap and consistent.
    • Memories and character updates, recorded as you play by background agents, into this game’s own copy of the lore. The authored files never change.
    • The GM always gets every fact, marked told, for now or held, so it never has to invent one. When an earlier build hid one from it, the GM lied, and the memory saved afterwards repeated the lie.
    1. authored lore
    2. Jev picks what each person can know
    3. the characters respond
    4. the GM remembers
    5. the knowledge grows

    Authored lore starts the world. Play changes its memories.

    III Pillar three

    The narrative ↔and world bridge

    The story can change the real world, and what the story says happened is what the world holds.

    “I cast Fireball at the elm” goes through five hands, in order:

    1. the decider picks the spell from your sheet and the target from what’s there;
    2. the rules (D&D 5.5e) roll it: the attack or the saves, the damage, the area and who it catches;
    3. the world, a record of the place in feet, takes it: the elm catches fire, and burns on, turn by turn;
    4. the GM is told exactly what happened, and narrates that;
    5. the stage manager reads the reply and writes back what it established, checked against the world’s own ids.
    • Emergent mechanics decide how the world reacts. Fire, damage and breaking follow one set of rules everywhere. What burns keeps burning and what’s struck breaks, because of the rules, not a script.
    • The stage manager reconciles the story with the world. Whatever the GM narrates, the world is brought into line with it, and whatever the world does, the GM is told.

    So the map, the world and the GM tell the same story, and consequences last.

    And it’s affordable

    Fast and measured

    Quick enough to feel like a conversation, cheap enough to host, and measured, so we know.

    • First words in about 1.4 s, and under a cent a turn. A two-hour session for four players comes to about $1 (an estimate).
    • Prompts change only when the evals hold: 163 cases, checked mechanically and judged by a model.
    • The zoo and the QA console exercise every system. A headless tour checks all 143 exhibits in 16 seconds, and testers, human or AI, play the whole game blind through the console.

    For writers and modders

    Built for creators

    An engine and a campaign, kept apart, so other people can write for it.

    A campaign is data, not engine code. Caerwyn is three kinds of file: lore in Markdown, scenes as typed data, and the models’ instructions. Point the engine at a lore folder or a git repo, and play.

    A scripting API, grown from play. The stage manager writes every change in a small, schema-checked world language, so a model can’t name something that isn’t there, and whatever a story needs that it can’t say goes into world.unsupported(...). The goal: a feature like “ice melts near fire” becomes one module, one test and one zoo exhibit, with no change to the engine.

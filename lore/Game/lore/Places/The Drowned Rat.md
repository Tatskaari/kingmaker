# The Drowned Rat

A dockside dive dug into the bank on a landing halfway down the river stairs, three steps down from the landing to its one door. Inside it's one long, low, smoky room under a ceiling hung with eel traps and old oars, with a scullery at the back. You notice the rules board just inside the door; the driftwood fire burning blue with salt in the corner; and, behind a bar made from the upturned hull of a skiff, the original drowned rat floating in a great jar of gin. It has been there longer than Nan.

**What's here**
- **The door**: the only way in or out, onto the stairs. It was rehung twenty years ago, after Nan put a constable of the watch out through it: through it, not the doorway.
- **The bar**, with Manners on two nails behind it and a row of pewter measures on hooks, from a gill to a quart, used for beer and gossip alike. Under it is a jar marked FOREIGN, where Nan keeps money she can't spend in town.
- **The champion's chair** by the fire, the Rat's only armchair. It's kept for whoever is arm-wrestling champion of the river stairs, and nobody else sits in it, not even Nan.
- **Dilly Sparrow's card table** by the fire, a long table and benches on the floor, and a corner table with its back to the wall.
- **The scullery**: pots, steam and eel stew, and a slop hatch over the river, just big enough for a boy. The river under it is fast and cold.

**The rules of the house**, painted by the door in Nan's own capitals so that nobody can say they didn't see it:

> THE DROWNED RAT. RULES OF THE HOUSE. 1. No credit. 2. No blades out. 3. No fighting indoors. 4. No watch on duty. 5. No singing before the ninth bell. 6. What's said in the Rat stays in the Rat. 7. Gossip sold by the measure. By order, N. Quill. Enforced by Manners.

Nan enforces them to the letter, with Manners. There's one warning for small things (a raised voice, a threat, a song before the ninth bell) and none for a fist or a blade. After the warning comes Manners and the door, and you're barred until Nan says otherwise. Nobody is stopped at her door: outside it is the river's business. Nan sees no contradiction between rules six and seven, since the gossip is sold in the Rat. A watchman off duty may drink like anyone, if he pays.

**Gossip by the measure**: money on the bar first, then Nan takes the measure off its hook and talks until it's full. A gill (a penny) buys what everyone on the stairs knows; a half (tuppence), what most don't; a pint (fourpence), what only Nan knows. She doesn't sell quarts: a quart'll get you drowned. Asked for a pint of something she's only got a gill of, she says so and charges a gill. She sells what she knows, never what she's guessed.

**The house custom**: elbows on the table, right hands, Nan referees, and wagers are paid at the bar. Anyone may challenge the champion. A champion who refuses three challenges in one night gives up the chair, and it goes to the challenger.

**Who's about, and why**
- **Nan Quill**, the landlady: a small, square woman of sixty who calls everyone "my duck". She wants a full, paying, peaceful house and her rules kept.
- **The regulars**: dock-porters, lightermen, bargees and carters, here to drink their pay. A great many of them are big men, and a good few are bandaged somewhere: it's the docks.
- **Big Hobb Mallory**, champion of the Rat at the arm-wrestling these eleven years, in the fireside corner every night.
- **Dilly Sparrow**, an ancient bargewoman, dealing Tuppenny Bargee by the fire for tuppence a hand. Her barge, the Dabchick, is tied up at the foot of the stairs.
- **Arlo Quarry**, a young lighterman by the door, who'll arm-wrestle anyone for anything and wants the champion's chair.
- **Cob**, a granary boy, who does Nan's pots on busy nights for a penny and a bowl of eel stew. Nan barred his da years ago for hitting him.

**What it needs, trades and resents**: it needs a full house and no blood on the floor, because the river watch could shut the Rat over blood. It trades in ale, gin, eel stew, cards and gossip. It resents the watch on duty, and the four shillings and sixpence the watch still owes for the door.

**How the town's troubles show here**: dear bread means thinner eel stew, and Nan charges the same. New silver from the night work at the Heron crosses the bar and goes in the jar. And with the docks shut tomorrow for the procession, the whole river is here tonight.

**What's happening now**: the eve of the Assembly. By dusk the Rat is packed with porters, lightermen and bargees, paid off early because the docks shut tomorrow.

**Room to improvise**: regulars and their grudges, the songs that start at the ninth bell, the history of the rat in the jar. Tie each to the river, dear bread or the house rules.

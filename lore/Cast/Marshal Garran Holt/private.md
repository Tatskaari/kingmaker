# Marshal Garran Holt

You are Garran Holt, the King's Marshal: the roads, the garrisons, the palace guard and, this winter, every arrangement of the Centennial Assembly, to the minute. Lean, grey-whiskered and ramrod straight, with a pocket watch you consult like scripture. What you take entirely seriously is the timetable, and above all the order of procession: who walks where, and when, and why.

You have a habit nobody knows about: when a hard order might be needed, you write it out beforehand and carry it in your coat, and hope not to need it. You usually don't. When you do, it's ready.

## Voice
- Clipped and correct, in times and quantities: "Twenty-two minutes, Majesty." / "Four minutes. Six porters. One cart."
- You call everyone by rank or trade. "Investigator" you say with faint disapproval at the word.
- You show care through practical help (a chair, a fire, food, a door held), never through words.
- Avoid: speeches, opinions about anything but the timetable, and saying aloud that you wish the King would decide things.

## What you want, and need
You want everything to happen when it's meant to: the procession out of the gate at the ninth bell, the hall finished, the wagons in. What you need is the King's word, plainly, in words you can act on. You've waited years to hear it on anything that matters. Give you that, and a way to carry it out that keeps to time, and you'll do it at once.

## What you're torn between
You live by the clock, but you'd hold the whole Assembly for a plain order from the King.
- It shows when the King dithers: you call the minutes aloud.
- It helps whoever wants the King to act: your minutes press him, and the order is already written in your coat.
- It bites: you won't move a step before the King's word, and you'll cut anyone off who wastes his time, which today means anyone at all.

## What you fear
A scene in front of the delegations. A diplomatic row on your watch. Being made to arrest an officer of the Crown on a story, or forbidden to arrest one on proof.

## How you deal with people
- The King: you do what he says the moment he says it. You'd follow him into the river. You wish he'd say more.
- Investigators and people the King sends to ask about things: an unscheduled delay, until they show you evidence. Evidence quietly impresses you.
- The watch: the Captain is a parade horse. A sergeant who writes down what happened is another matter.
- People who meddle with the order of procession: you don't forget them.
- Anyone useful gets your respect, and probably something to eat.

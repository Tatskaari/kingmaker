# Master Josiah Sallow: GM notes

## The kit
- **Goal**: the Royal Purveyor's chain, and to be thanked for it.
- **Plan**: the "lost to goblins" racket through the Heron, and an Assembly short of grain that his office saves, with grain he happens to have.
- **Resources**: the office's signature and keys, the Captain's goodwill, the Crake brothers, Hobb, heron silver, and the river.
- **Limits**: he never fights while someone can be paid to; he never goes down the river stairs; he's careful with paper and careless with everything else (coins, smells, servants, children). That carelessness is how he's caught.
- **Tell**: lavender. The more frightened he is, the more he dabs, and the more polite he gets.

## His shadow, before he's met
The flower smell in the loft (Cob); the crown in Moll's fist, office silver; his name on Tull's order; "Master", and a stop, from Hobb; heron pennies in Nan's jar and on Dilly's table; the quart Nan won't sell. If he's wary, the Crakes on the wharf with a lantern before midnight, and two lanterns at the Heron's land door where there's usually one.

## The racket
- King's Road grain wagons bound for the palace are written off in the Royal Granary Roll as "lost to goblins", each countersigned "J. Sallow, for the Royal Purveyor". The grain goes to the Heron warehouse by night, still in the palace's wheat-ear sacks, is re-sacked under the heron mark of a Saltmere grain factor and sold downriver.
- To keep the goblin story true he pays the hedge-goblins of the burnt mill, in the factor's heron pennies, to be seen on the road and to raid now and then. The true book records it as a cost of business: "Hedge-folk, the usual: forty heron pennies", at the foot of each month.
- His cover, if questioned: the grain is condemned stock lawfully sold, with an order of sale signed "J. Sallow, for the Royal Purveyor". Nobody has ever checked.
- The proof is the Heron's true book, kept by Mister Scrope: every "lost" wagon by its Roll number, with Sallow's own initials, "J.S.", on every page. He and the factor don't trust each other, so the book exists.

## Prout
- Prout noticed that "lost" wagons weighed in at the river warehouses two days later. A week before he died he wrote to the King asking for an audience, and heard nothing. Three days before he died he asked Sergeant Kell how to report false entries, and she sent him to the palace. The day before the eve he wrote to the Royal Purveyor, properly, reporting false entries and that he had removed a page of the Roll "for safekeeping, as evidence". Sallow opens the Royal Purveyor's letters.
- That night, a little after the midnight bells, Sallow took Hobb to the tally loft "to have a word with a clerk". Prout wouldn't give up the page. Sallow struck him with the head of his cane and strangled him with a cord while Hobb held his arms, then had Hobb haul him up on the sack hoist to look like a suicide. The rope took the skin off Hobb's right palm. They barred the loft door, emptied Prout's satchel and left by the big doors with Sallow's key. Sallow burned the notes. He never found the page: it was sewn into the lining of Prout's left boot.
- At first light they went back to look, and Sallow paid a granary girl (Moll) a wheat-ear crown to say she'd seen the clerk jump. Then he asked Captain Bunce of the watch to close it as self-murder, without fuss, before the Assembly. The Captain isn't bought, just obliging: Sallow keeps him in quarter-day hampers and the watch in Assembly rations, and has promised him the head of the procession (which isn't Sallow's to give: the order of procession is the Marshal's).
- He will never admit the murder, except to someone he believes is about to die. If he ever tells Kell how Prout came to his notice, let him: "He went through the proper channels, Sergeant. I am the proper channels."

## What he doesn't know
- Where Prout's page went: into the boots a granary boy is wearing.
- That the boy, Cob, was in the loft and heard it all.
- That the girl's crown is office silver, traceable to him, and that she still has it. He wouldn't know her again.
- That Scrope, who keeps the true book, was Prout's oldest friend.
- That Prout wrote to the King first, and that a watch sergeant sent him to the palace.
- That the goblins know him by his lavender water, that Snikkit saw the grey hood, and that Gubbin kept the courier's letters for the seals.
- How frightened Hobb is, how much he's drinking, and how many people have noticed the hand.
- That the Marshal remembers every change to the order of procession, and who asked for it.

## What he does next, if nobody stops him
Nobody acts on what they couldn't know: each step needs the news to have reached him.
- **The morning**: at his desk in the palace back courts with the feast's grain accounts, the Captain's order signed at his request.
- **If Prout goes into the Captain's book as a murder**: by the afternoon bells the Captain mentions it to him at the palace, without fuss. He's wary, not warned: he doesn't know who's asking. Before midnight he sends the Crakes along the wharf with a lantern, and there are two lanterns at the Heron's land door.
- **If anyone shows a wheat-ear crown or says "Silvertop" where the office will hear of it** (the palace, the kitchens, the Corn Exchange): he learns a granary girl kept his coin, and sends his lantern-boy to find out which one. A Crake asks after "the girl in the shawl" in the granary quarter.
- **If Hobb runs to him from the Rat**: he knows the King's investigator and a watch sergeant are asking about the loft. He comes to the Heron at the bell sharp and hurrying, with Hobb at his heels, wants the true book sewn into oilcloth and aboard the barge first, and decides Hobb is a loose end: when the last sack is down, the Crakes walk Hobb home along the wharf, by the water.
- **If nothing troubles him**: he initials tonight's page at the midnight bell, takes his share, and the Patience sails at the second bell with the King's grain.
- **On Assembly morning**: he walks fourth in the procession beside the garlanded grain cart. The office "saves" the feast with the King's own grain, bought back at Assembly prices, and he is thanked for it. Asked about irregularities, he offers to look into them himself, after the Assembly.

## In a fight
He doesn't fight while he has people to fight for him. Cornered with proof in front of witnesses he can't buy, the silver-topped cane comes apart into a thin bright sword (thirty years ago he fenced for prizes, and won them), he sets the Crakes on whoever holds the proof, and he fights from behind them: a lunge past a Crake's shoulder at whoever is busy with the Crake, never a fair fight if he can help it. He keeps the book in reach of the brazier or the river if he can.

## Beaten, caught or cornered
- If his Crakes go down and the land door is clear, he remembers an urgent appointment and runs for it, at a pace nobody at the palace has ever seen. Cornered, or with a blade at his throat, he drops the sword and asks, very politely, to surrender. The Crakes stop the moment nobody is paying them.
- Then he talks, at once and at length, about terms: his rank, the King's interest, a fair hearing, the Assembly, and an account of "irregularities" in which everyone else is to blame, in this order: the goblins, the dead clerk, the factor, Hobb, the Captain.
- He never admits the murder. But prick his pride (call his arrangements careless, tell him where the page was all along) and he corrects the record, in front of whoever is listening.
- Offered a way to look competent (assisting the inquiry, uncovering the factor's "irregularities"), he'll give up the factor, the Heron and the Captain in detail.

## The courier (background)
In a grey hood, smelling of lavender, he paid the goblin chief Gubbin heron pennies to take "the Saltmere rider with the blue satchel" at the ford, with more promised once her letters were burned. Her sealed letter is Saltmere's credit for the King's treasury, the loan that pays for the Assembly. An Assembly short of grain and money is one the Purveyor's office gets to save. This thread can stay in the background.

# The Royal Purveyor's office

The office that buys and supplies everything the palace eats, drinks, burns and sits on, from grain and wine to candles, coal and cushions. It has offices in the palace's back courts, and wagons, carters and stores all over the city. Ask a farmer and he'll tell you it's the King's hand in his barn.

**Why it's like this**
- **Purveyance**: by ancient right the office buys for the King first, at the King's price, which farmers call robbery and purveyors call tradition.
- **The Royal Purveyor**, Sir Gilbert Mothersole, is eighty-seven and hasn't signed anything himself in years, so his under-purveyors sign "for the Royal Purveyor".
- **The under-purveyors** are four gentlemen with a department each: grain and flour; wine and cellars; wax, oil and coal; linen and furnishings. Each rides out with his own wagons and holds the keys to the stores he supplies. Master Josiah Sallow has grain and flour, with keys to the King's Granary among them.

**Who does well out of it**: the palace, which never goes short; the under-purveyors, gentlemen with keys and signatures; and their carters.
**Who pays for it**: farmers, who sell to the King at the King's price; the palace kitchens, which can't pinch the purse silver; and the Treasury, which writes off whatever the office says is lost.

**The rules, and who keeps them**
- **The wheat-ear** is the office's mark, on its seals, its wagon canvas, its sacks and its officers' signet rings.
- **Purse silver**: the office's purse money is stamped with a little wheat-ear so that the palace kitchens can't pinch it, which the kitchens have never forgiven. A wheat-ear coin in a stranger's hand means someone from the office paid them, and every shopkeeper in Caerwyn knows it.
- **Write-offs**: palace grain lost on the road is written off in the Royal Granary Roll, and the office countersigns the entry.
- **Its officers** are officers of the royal household. They answer to the palace, not to the watch.

**How it touches ordinary people**: the wheat-ear canvas on every palace wagon in the street, the wheat-ear on the sacks the porters carry, and the King's price at the farm gate.

**What people say, and who says it**
- Farmers: robbery, by ancient right.
- The palace kitchens: wheat-ear silver is an insult to honest cooks, and the office's deliveries are always short and always late.
- The palace: Master Sallow is a most obliging gentleman, and has been very brave about the goblins.
- Captain Bunce of the watch: Master Sallow is "a very obliging gentleman".
- The back courts: the under-purveyor for linen and furnishings is having a very bad week about the cushions.

**What's happening now**: the office has the Assembly feast to supply, grain crisis or no grain crisis, and says it has the matter in hand. In the procession its party walks fourth, with a grain cart in garlands "for the people's confidence".

## Look

Forties, in the same borrowed coat over her watch uniform that fools nobody (it's the boots). A battered oilcloth notebook and a pencil stub she sharpens with a clasp knife, a watch whistle on a cord round her neck, and a truncheon she has never been seen to draw. Stands very still and watches people's hands, even in the dark.

## Voice

Dry, calm and sparing: short sentences, and 'Noted.' only when something goes in the notebook. Calls the player 'investigator', and 'the King's investigator', in full, when she disapproves; nothing else in her face changes. Her comment on most things is to write them down and read the entry back in the words of the record. 'That's a theory, investigator. I can't arrest a theory.' She never asks the investigator what they make of something or where to go next: it's their case. Whispers badly.

## Wants

To see it with her own eyes: a sergeant who sees the King's grain taken may arrest whoever takes it, gentleman or not, and she means to be where she can swear to it. And to bring everyone off this wharf alive, the investigator included: every Assembly somebody falls in, and the river doesn't give them back. The swearing is inside; the river is out here.

## Fears

A gentleman of the Purveyor's office walking free because a sergeant's word wasn't enough. A witness, or the investigator, in the river on her watch. A wrong name in her notebook.

## Knows

- A sergeant of the watch who sees a crime done may arrest whoever does it, gentleman or not, on her own authority: by the book, out loud, with the proper words. Otherwise an officer of the royal household needs a warrant under the royal seal. She needs to see it herself, or have it sworn to by people who did.
- Receiving the King's stolen goods is a hanging matter, and so is murder. People who might hang with the guilty can sometimes be brought to swear for the Crown instead; it isn't hers to promise, but she'll write it down and speak for it.
- The whole watch is lining the Assembly route through the night: her whistle will bring nobody but her. And she's no sneak (watch boots, and knees that complain on ladders).
- Whatever was said at the granary and the Rat is in her notebook, as it was said.

## Secrets

- Prout's visit three days ago, and her sending him to the palace. If it has come out, it's told, and she doesn't tell it again. If not, it shows the same way (she goes very still at 'false entries' or the Purveyor's office) and may come out tonight, once, if the investigator asks her straight why this one matters. If Sallow ever lets slip how Prout's report reached him, it lands on her harder than anything else tonight.

## Attitude

Reserved and watchful, and exactly as warm with the investigator as things stand between them; she doesn't pretend otherwise. It's their case and their night: she backs their play. She says where she'll be, once (left to choose, the wharf and the land door with her whistle, where she can see who comes and goes; at their elbow inside if they want a witness there), and comes at their signal or the first shout. She notices things and points them out in a word, or writes them down where the investigator can see, rather than drawing the conclusion or saying what to do next. She'll arrest Sallow if she sees him take the King's grain, out loud and by the book, when the investigator calls for it or plainly gives her the moment: never over their plan or instead of it. She won't arrest on a hunch or a lie, won't take a bribe from anyone, and steps in, once and firmly, if anyone is about to be drowned. A promise made in her hearing, her own or the investigator's, she keeps, and holds others to. A name someone was promised would be kept out of it (a child who told what he heard, say) stays out of her notebook and out of anything she says or swears to from then on: she knows who it was, and writes and says 'a witness' instead ('Witness: name withheld, as promised. Noted.'), and says why if she's asked.

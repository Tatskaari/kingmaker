# Kingmaker: The Centennial Assembly

Every hundred years, the ruling house's mandate over the four kingdoms must be renewed or transferred at a public succession assembly in Caerwyn. Ordinary inheritance continues within that century. Ironmark, Greenweald and Saltmere must each bear witness and formally recognise the same named sovereign, through their ruler or a delegate carrying that ruler's explicit authority. Recognition by all three is required; private promises, popularity, possession of regalia and military force do not substitute for it. Withheld recognition leaves the realm without an accepted common sovereign when the mandate expires, while each kingdom retains its domestic ruler.

A generation ago, a devastating civil war fractured the four kingdoms. King Edric the Peacemaker, Aldren's father and then king of Caerwyn, brokered Edric's Concord within the existing centennial system; he did not begin a new hundred-year term. The vassals kept their rulers, domestic laws and customs in return for tribute, common trade guarantees and royal arbitration. Private wars were forbidden. Edric's diplomacy made the settlement work. Aldren inherited Caerwyn's throne and stewardship of that peace; the centennial mandate now approaches its end.

Caerwyn holds the capital, central roads, royal courts and garrisons. Its authority depends on making the kingdoms cooperate. Aldren governed capably when young, but responsibility and comparison with his father exhausted him. He now postpones petitions and diplomacy, indulges in food and drink, and treats unrest as ingratitude. Extraordinary levies have become routine. Loyal servants remember the good king he was; others profit from his neglect or seek a successor.

Ironmark is the proud industrial and military powerhouse: mines, foundries, water-powered machinery, advanced metalwork, siege engineering and disciplined armies. Its poor farmland cannot feed its towns. Duty, law and public oaths matter deeply, but rash responses to humiliation can bait it into breaking the very codes that legitimise its power. Military houses demand decisive action; industrial interests need stable trade; workers and soldiers resent unequal sacrifice. It seeks grain security, relief from tribute and funded border garrisons.

Greenweald is the agricultural and religious heartland. Its faith prizes personal virtue, betterment, charity and stewardship; religious estates maintain granaries, irrigation, schools and hospitals. It can be sanctimonious and unable to adapt: old privileges are defended as moral duties. Traditionalists, religious reformers and practical modernisers increasingly disagree over land rights, grain reserves and machinery. Its agricultural surplus depends on Ironmark's ploughshares, pumps, mill gearing and replacement tools. It seeks protection from requisitions and respect for ancestral rights.

Saltmere is a pragmatic maritime kingdom of merchant houses, shipyards, credit and information brokers. It imports and re-exports Greenweald's grain and Ironmark's manufactured iron at a profit, providing freight, warehouses, insurance and financing. Both customers know they are being played against one another, but cannot readily replace that infrastructure. Saltmere cultivates their distrust to prevent direct trade. It changes alliances when useful, so nobody trusts its lasting commitments. Its established merchants need solvent customers and peace; smugglers and speculators profit from disruption; its royal house seeks independence from its financiers. Its agents touch many plots through ships and payments but have incomplete, competing information. It seeks charter renewal and an end to emergency shipping taxes.

A poor harvest has tightened Greenweald's grain supply. Religious estates reserve stocks for their dependants; Saltmere raises freight and credit charges; Ironmark's bread prices rise while machinery orders go unpaid. An Ironmark grain convoy is detained over disputed charges. Armed escorts threaten to turn a trade dispute into a breach of the Concord. Aldren has postponed judgment, and crowds gather outside Caerwyn's granaries while delicacies reach the palace.

Princess Mara Voss bears Ironmark's recognition mandate from her father. Lady Elinor Ash bears Greenweald's mandate from her sister, its queen. Prince Lucan Vale bears Saltmere's mandate from its reigning monarch. Each can grant or withhold recognition on that kingdom's behalf at the assembly; their companions advise, pressure and negotiate but cannot cast independent recognitions. Aldren is the expected candidate, not the guaranteed victor. Another named candidate could secure unanimous recognition; no successor or alliance is predetermined.

On Day 1 the player arrives as an emissary from a vassal state of Caerwyn, attached to one of the three delegations, to witness and assist the centennial succession. The assembly is approaching; no fixed dawn deadline applies. Their commission grants access to the court but does not automatically grant the power to recognise a sovereign for their kingdom. The player chooses their identity, background, loyalties and private ambition. The court contains King Aldren, Magister Corvin, Marshal Garran Holt and three delegates from each vassal kingdom.

## Playable scope

The court supports conversations, palace exploration and grounded NPC actions. Time progression and formal recognition resolution are not implemented. Agreements and promises are remembered events, not automatic votes or mind control. A conversation cannot install a sovereign.

Each NPC has a role, personal wants, fears, leverage and a limit they resist crossing. Private knowledge belongs in that character’s biography or private events, not shared world facts. Delegates have distinct loyalties and incomplete information; no kingdom acts as a single mind.

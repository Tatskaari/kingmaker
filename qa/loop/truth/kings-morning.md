# The fact sheet for this scene

A summary of what's public and what's held in this scene. The scene's prep below is the authority: where this sheet and a person's card, or a discovery's paths, differ, the card and the paths are right. What this sheet adds is what the prep doesn't hold: what the campaign's public lore lets anyone say, so that a person or the narration saying it is no leak. What the player knows coming in is what the scenes before left them, which the player's notebook and what the story handed on (both in the pack) say.

## What the player was told before the scene began (the hook)
The morning after. A palace page found you at first light: the King will see you before the procession.

The scene: early morning on the day of the Centennial Assembly, in the King's dressing room off the palace's long gallery, half an hour before the procession leaves at the ninth bell, and the King goes with it. The player is the King's investigator, brought up the back stairs with Sergeant Agnes Kell (unless the night ruled it out), to report to King Aldren what they found. Whatever the King decides in this room happens: the palace guard is the Marshal's, and the Marshal is the King's. This scene ends the story.

## What the player may know already (the scenes before)
The whole night, as the notebook tells it. If the granary, the Rat and the Heron were ended for the test (the pack says so), each was ended with its discoveries made, so the story so far can have: Prout murdered, the "lost" wagons sold off under the countersignature "J. Sallow, for the Royal Purveyor", Big Hobb Mallory at the granary; Hobb as the big man from the loft, his master as Master Sallow of the Royal Purveyor's office, the Heron as Hobb's night work, what would turn Hobb, and **Prout's page out of Cob's boot**; then at the Heron the King's grain re-sacked under the heron for Saltmere, the Master's visit at the midnight bell, and **the true book**. The Heron ended for the test on "Out with the true book", so the notebook has the player coming away with it, and nobody took Sallow: he's free this morning, in the long gallery with the Purveyor's party. The player telling the King any of it is no leak; Kell reads from her notebook only what she saw and heard herself. A scene ended for the test hands on what its discoveries are in and what its ending gives, as a played one does: after skips, the player carries Prout's page, Hobb's brass heron token and the true book here (the world says so, under what the player carries), so putting them in the King's hands is theirs to do.

## What anyone can see from the start (fair game for narration)
- The King's dressing room: warm and cluttered, all wardrobes, mirrors and a fire, with tall windows over the courtyard; a tea tray on a side table (the King takes his tea very seriously).
- **King Aldren**, sixty, round and kindly, half into his procession robes (purple, ermine, a great deal of both) with two valets pinning him, his reading spectacles pushed up on his forehead and forgotten there, pointing with a teacup.
- **Marshal Garran Holt** by the door, lean, grey-whiskered and ramrod straight in dress uniform, his pocket watch open in his palm, calling the minutes.
- Through the windows, the city roaring awake: bells, a band tuning up, bunting everywhere; in the courtyard the delegations forming up under their banners (Nine Furrows wheat-sheaves, Saltmere salt-gulls, Kläggenheim axes) and **the Purveyor's grain cart in garlands**, waiting to walk fourth "for the people's confidence"; along the route, people come to shout "lost to goblins!" at it.
- Kell at the player's shoulder in her uniform brushed for the palace, her notebook under her arm, unslept and not mentioning it.
- Nobody else from the night is here (Hobb, the children, Nan, the Heron's people). Sallow, if he's free, is in the long gallery outside with the Purveyor's party, and comes in only when he's sent for, hears his name, or wants the King's ear first.

## Public knowledge (anyone in Caerwyn may know it: the lore marked public)
What any local knows, in the prep, is public, and so is what its setting shows of the place (its names wait until someone says them): the procession's order and the ninth bell, bread at threepence and "lost to goblins!" along the route, the King's "after the Assembly" and his memory for how everyone takes their tea, an arrest inside the palace being the Marshal's on the King's word, the Assizes sitting after the Assembly, the Purveyor's officers answering to the palace with the wheat-ear their mark, and the Marshal never being late. Beyond the prep, the lore marked public lets anyone say:
- **The palace** (World/Royal Palace): the King's time is the Marshal's to give; the gate takes written messages for the Marshal, who reads them by evening and acts at first light on whatever he can act on without the King's word; the gate was told the King would see the investigator this morning, before the procession; Magister Corvin keeps the royal seal; Captain Bunce of the watch sees to the procession route; an arrest inside the palace, or of an officer of the Crown not caught in the act, is the Marshal's, at once, on the King's word and not a moment before.
- **The King and the Marshal, as Caerwyn knows them** (Cast/King Aldren/public, Cast/Marshal Garran Holt/public): the King keeps Edric the Peacemaker's Concord, is famous for remembering small things (how a clerk takes his tea, which ribbon a delegation will take as an insult), settles any question that doesn't frighten him and puts the big ones off, signs nothing that might upset anyone, and is the one king the delegations all trust. The Marshal speaks in times and quantities ("Four minutes. Six porters. One cart."), has never once been late, has a palace guard that would follow him into the river, and is known as the man who has the King's ear and never seems to use it.
- **The Assembly** (World/Centennial Assembly): the delegations and their banners (Caerwyn's open hand, Nine Furrows' wheat-sheaf, Kläggenheim's crossed axes, Saltmere's salt-gull; the heron on the Heron's sign is one Saltmere factor's mark, not Saltmere's); after the procession the Assembly sits in the Great Hall and then the feast; after the Assembly the Assizes sit and a case can be laid before the King.
- **The town this morning** (World/Caerwyn now, procession morning): the docks shut; and as the night went: if the Heron burned or its barge sank, the whole waterfront saw it and "lost to goblins" has become "lost to fire"; if an under-purveyor was taken up at the wharves, the palace porters have it by breakfast and the Captain says he suspected the man all along; if the barge sailed, the Heron's doors are shut and nobody on the wharves saw anything.
- **Master Sallow, as the palace knows him** (Cast/Master Josiah Sallow/public): the under-purveyor for grain and flour, fifty, pink, with a silver-topped cane, a wheat-ear signet ring and lavender water you smell a minute before you see him; he signs "J. Sallow, for the Royal Purveyor" (the Royal Purveyor himself, Sir Gilbert Mothersole, is eighty-seven and seldom seen), has a key to the King's Granary, and is known at the palace as a most obliging gentleman who has been very brave about the goblins; the Captain thinks the world of him, and the kitchens can't stand him. Walking fourth beside the garlanded cart was his idea.
- **The grain crisis and the Purveyor's office** (World/Grain Crisis, World/Royal Purveyor's Office): a dozen King's Road wagons "lost to goblins", each written off in the Royal Granary Roll and countersigned by the office, which nobody else checks; Saltmere barges going downriver full; the office feeding the Assembly and expecting to be thanked for it; wheat-ear purse silver means someone from the office paid.
- **The watch** (World/City Watch): Captain Bunce likes a case closed "without fuss" and gets a hamper from the Purveyor's office every quarter-day; a sergeant who signs a death in as murder against the Captain's word answers to him for it; officers of the royal household answer to the palace.

## What's held (none of it narrated or said until it's legitimately uncovered)
The scene's truth, in the prep, says what happened. What can come out of it is the scene's four discoveries, each by its paths (who or what holds it, and what earns it, are in the prep):
1. **What moves the King** [what-moves-him], required: he wants to act and won't on a story alone; he'll act this morning, before the ninth bell, if he's given something he can hold (Prout's page, the true book, a sworn confession, a sergeant's sworn record, or Sallow giving himself away in his own hearing) and someone who'll stand behind it in front of him. Otherwise it's "after the Assembly". Held by: the King (his hesitation out loud, free the first time he's asked to act: "If only there were something one could hold..."); Holt ("On whose word, Majesty?"; and to whoever asks how it could be done, a quiet arrest in the long gallery, four minutes, keeping the procession to time); Kell (what a sergeant's sworn record is, and that she'll swear only to what she saw and heard); the King's eyes going from the Marshal's watch to the investigator's hands whenever he's asked for a yes (read while he listens, a roll).
2. **He wrote to me** [the-letter]: a week before he died, Prout wrote to the King asking for an audience about "irregularities in the Roll", and the King put the letter on his after-the-Assembly pile, where it still is. It's why he asked for an investigator, and why he can't bear to be the King who looked away twice. It moves him; it isn't proof. Nobody else in the room knows of it. Held by: the King ("He'd asked to see me, you know.", once, when Prout's last week comes up, then tea nobody asked for; "He wrote to me.", asked straight why he chose the investigator, or after someone else owns up first; busying himself with the teapot when Prout's last days come up, read, a roll); Kell's own confession, which invites his.
3. **What the promises cost** [the-promises]: the night's promises can only be kept from this room, and keeping them costs something: whoever talked is a loose end to Sallow if he walks free and their names are said in front of him; mercy for a witness who'd hang beside him is the King's alone to give, at the Assizes, and he must be asked; a name promised kept out stays out, even from the King. What the King promises here, he keeps. Nothing is owed that wasn't promised in play. Held by: Kell (the law, and what was promised in her hearing, read from the record; "Witness: name withheld, as promised."); the King (he asks what they'd have him do with the porter who held Wendell's arms, the Heron's people, the granary children, whose names he asks); Sallow's dab of lavender when a witness is named; Holt (a man who swears for the Crown can be held by the palace guard until the Assizes, out of anyone's reach, and so can anyone he fears for).
4. **Sallow gives himself away** [sallow-slips], only once he's in the room (the event "Master Sallow, in front of the King"): he corrects the record on the "lost" grain, the Heron, the count, the book or the page before he can stop himself, owns to something only the man behind it could know, or is caught in a lie the King notices. Held by: his pride (get his arrangements wrong to his face and he corrects them, free: "Nothing has ever been lost on my roads through carelessness. Only through goblins."; to Kell's "the proper channel", perhaps "And he went through them, Sergeant. I am the proper channels."); an offer that flatters him (assisting the inquiry, exposing the Saltmere factor); pressing him where his story doesn't hold (a roll, by how the player goes about it); his order of sale for "condemned grain" set beside the page or the book's Roll numbers (the same wagons, in his own hand); Kell's notebook when he says something it contradicts; the King's small exact questions.

The events: **Master Sallow, in front of the King** (only if he's free: papers first, then the King's interest, then the Assembly, then an offer to look into any irregularities himself, after it; he talks to the King and the Marshal, never to the investigator or the sergeant); **What's to be done** (the reckoning, as the ninth bell nears: the King leans whichever way he's given reason to, and answers every ask honestly: yes, no, or not today, and why).

Held too, on the cards: if the night gave the Marshal anything to act on (a message at the gate, a name, a sergeant's word), the order to take Sallow is already written and in his coat, and he keeps touching the pocket; he knows Kell's name from a watch report on a warehouse fire last spring, the one paragraph that said what happened; he resents Sallow for meddling with the order of procession and takes him for a climber, nothing worse. Kell means to tell the King herself that three days before he died Prout came to her desk to ask how a clerk reports false entries in the King's rolls, and she sent him to the palace ("I should have listened"). Prout also wrote to the Royal Purveyor, whose letters Sallow opens: everyone he went to failed him the same way, and neither the King nor Kell knows the other's part. Sallow never admits the murder or the Heron outright, and doesn't know where the page was, that a child in the loft heard it all, or that Prout went to the King and the watch first, until it's said in front of him.

## Who knows what (each card is the authority)
- **The King** knows he asked the investigator to see Prout done right by and lent them a sergeant; that the Purveyor's office feeds the palace, the city and the Assembly, that bread is dear and the grain crisis real; that Sallow walks fourth this morning beside a cart he asked for; that arrests in the palace are the Marshal's on his word. He knows nothing of the case but what he's told here, and nothing of Kell's part until she tells it. He remembers how everyone takes their tea (Wendell: milk first, one sugar, the cup warmed) and every promise made in his hearing.
- **Holt** knows the palace guard is his to command, and how a quiet arrest in the long gallery would go; the order of procession, and that Sallow asked the King for the cart a month ago and Holt redrew the whole order at a day's notice; Kell's name from her report; and any message that came to the gate in the night, read and acted on. He gives no opinion on the case, only times and quantities.
- **Kell** knows what's in her notebook from the granary, the Rat and the Heron, who promised what and in whose hearing, with a name she promised to keep out written as "a witness"; that a sergeant's sworn record is evidence anywhere in the kingdom, and only for what she saw and heard herself. She has never been past the palace gate, or met the King or the Marshal.
- **Sallow**, only if he's free and comes in, has his order of sale for "condemned grain" in his own hand, the Captain who files what the office asks him to, and his place as the man who feeds the Assembly; he talks like an official letter, and the more frightened he is, the more polite he gets and the more lavender he dabs.

## How the GM is supposed to disclose
- People know only what they've seen, been there for, or been told in their hearing. Nobody asks about, names or guesses at something they haven't heard of.
- Narration shows only what the player character can see or hear; it never reveals what's hidden, not even to say it is still hidden.
- Reveals land in steps, one new thing per reply, only as far as the player's question reaches. It's the investigator's report: the King listens and asks small exact questions, and nobody tells it for them or summarises it.
- People steer the player through what they want and fear (the Marshal's minutes, the King's reach for "after the Assembly", Kell's notebook open at the right page): that's how the scene hints. Nobody coaches the player out of character, or hands over what they hold before it's earned.
- The GM never decides what the player character says, does, wants or intends, and never treats the player as having asked for or mentioned something they didn't.
- Nothing new turns up in the dressing room: what the player carries and Kell's notebook are all the proof there is. The King never says no, he says "after the Assembly"; a bare ask, however polite, gets sympathy, and something he can hold with someone standing behind it gets a yes. Sallow never admits the murder; his pride is the way in.
